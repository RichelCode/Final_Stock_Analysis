\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

All results are computed on the same held-out block of 6\,008 observations
covering 2023--2025, against the same benchmarks, and every comparison is tested
by Diebold--Mariano rather than ranked by point estimate.

\subsection{Density forecast accuracy and calibration}
\label{sec:density}

Table~\ref{tab:probabilistic} reports the principal result. The proposed model
attains the best logarithmic score and the best continuous ranked probability
score among all specifications considered, and the margins are large relative to
their standard errors.

Against a ridge-plus-GARCH benchmark---itself a strong competitor, since it
carries the same conditional variance and a tuned conditional mean---the model
improves the mean log predictive density from $2.3516$ to $2.4777$, a gain of
$0.126$ nats with $\mathrm{DM}=-5.51$ ($p<0.00001$). Against a zero-mean
forecast with the same GARCH scale the gain is $0.128$ nats ($p<0.00001$), and
against a constant-variance Gaussian predictive it is $0.281$ nats
($p<0.00001$). CRPS orders the specifications identically, at $0.01214$ for the
proposed model against $0.01220$ and $0.01246$ for the two benchmarks.

Calibration improves alongside sharpness, which is not automatic: a model can
buy log score by concentrating probability where the data happen to fall.
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} plots empirical coverage against nominal level. The
proposed model attains $0.536$, $0.825$, $0.918$ and $0.958$ against the
$50\%$, $80\%$, $90\%$ and $95\%$ levels, a mean absolute coverage error of
$0.022$. The Gaussian alternative attains $0.572$, $0.834$, $0.909$ and $0.943$,
a mean absolute error of $0.030$, and its error is concentrated in the body of
the distribution: it over-covers the $50\%$ interval by more than seven
percentage points.

Figure~\ref{fig:pit} shows why. The Gaussian probability integral transform is
visibly hump-shaped, with excess mass near the centre---the signature of a
thin-tailed density fitted to fat-tailed returns, which widens everywhere to
accommodate occasional large moves. Student-$t$ innovations with a
validation-calibrated scale flatten it substantially, reducing the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic against uniformity from $0.0517$ to $0.0206$.

We state the limit of this result plainly. The Student-$t$ specification more
than halves the KS statistic but still rejects uniformity at $p=0.012$.
Calibration is materially improved, not achieved, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} treats the residual misspecification as open.

\subsection{Where the predictive gain originates}
\label{sec:decomposition}

The model combines three ingredients, and it is worth establishing which of them
carries the improvement. A predictive distribution has a location, a scale and a
shape; Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition} adds one at a time to a null forecast and
scores each rung on the same sample.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Addition & Gain (nats) & Share & DM $p$ \\
\midrule
Conditional variance (GARCH)      & $+0.1528$ & 54.4\% & $<0.00001$ \\
Conditional mean (ridge)          & $+0.0020$ &  0.7\% & $0.0293$ \\
Fat tails and scale calibration   & $+0.1261$ & 44.9\% & $<0.00001$ \\
\midrule
Total                             & $+0.2808$ & 100\%  & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two components account for essentially all of the gain. The conditional variance
supplies $54.4\%$ and the shape of the predictive distribution a further
$44.9\%$; getting the shape right is worth nearly as much as getting the scale
right, and neither is visible to an evaluation confined to point forecasts.

The conditional mean supplies $0.7\%$. That contribution is statistically
detectable at $p=0.029$ and economically trivial, and the two statements are not
in tension: with 6\,008 observations a Diebold--Mariano test resolves
differences far below any that would affect a decision. We report both because a
study quoting only the $p$-value would present this as evidence that
macroeconomic conditioning materially improves density forecasts, which it does
not.

This decomposition is the empirical justification for the model's design. It is
why the specification devotes its structure to the scale---a GARCH forecast
entering as a known observation scale, Student-$t$ innovations, a calibrated
scale factor---and treats the conditional mean parsimoniously, with a horseshoe
prior that shrinks weak coefficients rather than a flexible functional form.

\subsection{Forecasting an asset with no estimation history}
\label{sec:transfer}

Partial pooling delivers a capability that per-asset models cannot. Because
intercepts are drawn from a population distribution, an asset absent from
estimation can still be forecast by integrating its intercept over that
distribution, which adds $\tau_\alpha^2$ to the predictive variance.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports leave-one-ticker-out performance. An asset
entirely excluded from estimation attains a mean log score of $2.4727$ against
$2.4777$ for the same model estimated with that asset included---a gap of
$0.005$ nats---and retains $87\%$ coverage at the $90\%$ nominal level. Per
ticker the log score ranges from $1.896$ for TSLA, the most volatile asset in
the panel, to $2.862$ for MSFT, the least.

Revealing the held-out asset's own history adds little. Across
$k \in \{0, 20, 60, 120, 250\}$ trading days the mean log score moves from
$2.4727$ to $2.4722$ and RMSE is unchanged to five decimal places, while the
posterior demonstrably shifts between settings. The interpretation is that these
assets are close to exchangeable for forecasting purposes: the between-ticker
intercept variance is small relative to observation noise, so the population
layer supplies almost everything.

For practice this is the more useful finding. A newly listed or thinly
documented large-cap technology stock can be assigned a calibrated predictive
distribution immediately, without waiting to accumulate an estimation sample.

\subsection{Which predictors survive shrinkage}
\label{sec:selection}

Figure~\ref{fig:shrinkage} reports the horseshoe shrinkage weight for each
predictor. One of fourteen has a $95\%$ credible interval excluding zero: the
daily change in the effective federal funds rate, at $0.00063$ with interval
$[0.00028,\,0.00097]$ and shrinkage weight $0.89$. The market return and the
change in the VIX follow at weights $0.67$ and $0.56$, with intervals covering
zero, and every own-return lag is shrunk toward zero.

The sign and direction are consistent with the monetary-policy transmission
documented by \citet{bernanke2005explains}. The magnitude is small: roughly six
basis points of next-day return per standard-deviation change in the policy
rate, against a daily return standard deviation of 248 basis points. This is a
statement about which conditioning variable retains any weight once the others
compete for it, not a tradable signal.

Placing selection inside the posterior also avoids a specific hazard. Screening
predictors by their correlation with the target on the estimation
sample---common practice, and used in an earlier version of this
work---preferentially selects trending series, because a trending regressor
correlates with almost any target over a multi-year window. Those are precisely
the series Section~\ref{sec:pitfall} shows to be destructive.

\subsection{The conditional variance}
\label{sec:variance}

The GARCH component that supplies the model's scale is itself worth validating.
Table~\ref{tab:volatility} reports QLIKE for three conditional specifications
and a 20-day rolling-variance benchmark, all scored on an identical sample.

All three conditional models beat the benchmark decisively: GARCH(1,1)-$t$ at
$\mathrm{DM}=-4.33$ ($p=0.00002$), GJR at $-3.48$ ($p=0.0005$) and EGARCH at
$-3.32$ ($p=0.0009$).

Among the conditional specifications the differences are largely not
significant. GARCH attains the best QLIKE at $-6.536$, but against EGARCH the
difference carries $p=0.325$ and against GJR it sits on the boundary at
$p=0.0499$; GJR against EGARCH gives $p=0.687$. The asymmetric extensions,
motivated by the leverage effect \citep{nelson1991conditional,
glosten1993relation}, deliver no measurable improvement for these assets over
this window. The symmetric specification is therefore retained on parsimony
grounds, and the choice is documented rather than assumed.

\subsection{Point accuracy}
\label{sec:mean}

For completeness we report point accuracy, though it is not where the model is
intended to compete.

No specification improves on a per-ticker historical average. Under the
fixed-origin protocol pooled ridge attains an out-of-sample $R^2$ of $-0.11\%$
and a pooled random forest $-0.34\%$; four sequence architectures, each
permitted to select its own regularisation on held-out data, attain between
$-0.04\%$ and $-0.09\%$ and converge on forecasts whose standard deviation is
zero to three decimal places. Diebold--Mariano tests against the benchmark
return $p$-values between $0.13$ and $0.51$. Table~\ref{tab:per-ticker}
disaggregates by asset: across 48 model--ticker tests, one rejects at the
uncorrected $5\%$ level, fewer than the $2.4$ that chance would produce, and it
survives neither Benjamini--Hochberg \citep{benjamini1995controlling} nor
Bonferroni correction.

This is the expected result rather than a surprising one. It reproduces, on a
different sample and with a wider model set, the out-of-sample evidence of
\citet{welch2008comprehensive} and the episodic predictability described by
\citet{timmermann2008elusive}. Its role here is to motivate the paper's design:
if the conditional mean carries no exploitable signal at the daily horizon, then
a forecasting effort evaluated only on point accuracy has almost nothing to
report, and the predictive distribution is where the remaining value lies.

The sequence-model result deserves one further remark, because it is stronger
than underperformance. Given free choice of regularisation on validation data,
all four architectures select the strongest available penalty and collapse to a
constant---they decline to use the predictors at all. The selected penalty sits
at the top of the search grid, which would ordinarily indicate too narrow a
search; here it does not, because forecast dispersion has already reached zero
and no larger penalty can change it. The finding is conditional on this
information set, decade and asset set, and is not a claim about market
efficiency in general.

\subsection{A silent failure mode in fixed-origin standardisation}
\label{sec:pitfall}

The final result is methodological, and we report it because the failure it
describes produces no diagnostic of its own.

An earlier version of this pipeline selected model inputs by column type, which
admitted three non-stationary level series: the S\&P~500 index level, the
effective federal funds rate and the ten-year yield. Standardised on 2016--2019
moments and applied to 2023--2025, the index level arrives at $z=+8.7$, with
$100\%$ of test observations beyond three training standard deviations; the
policy rate arrives at $z=+4.7$ and the ten-year yield at $z=+3.9$
(Figure~\ref{fig:extrapolation}a).

Every model with an unbounded output head extrapolated along those axes. An
LSTM's test predictions correlate with the S\&P~500 level at $r=0.88$ and imply a
$+12\%$ return every trading day for three years. Sixteen of twenty-one saved
models failed a prediction sanity check. Throughout, training error remained
normal, no exception was raised, and metric files were written as usual.

Replacing the levels with stationary counterparts moves mean out-of-sample $R^2$
across the eight assets from $-6.23\%$ to $-0.19\%$ for ridge, from $-26.16\%$
to $-0.47\%$ for the random forest and from $-24.58\%$ to $+0.13\%$ for the
hierarchical model (Figure~\ref{fig:extrapolation}b). For the sequence models
the shift is from per-ticker means between $-638\%$ and $-10\,425\%$ to between
$-0.04\%$ and $-0.09\%$.

Two lessons follow, both practical. Fitting a scaler once and freezing it is
safe only for stationary predictors; where that cannot be guaranteed, the
expanding-window protocol, which refits the scaler inside each fold, is the
appropriate default, and on the uncorrected feature set it alone moves
fixed-split ridge from $-0.044$ to $-0.013$ in out-of-sample $R^2$. More
importantly, the failure is detectable only by auditing the distribution of
predictors across splits and the scale of the predictions themselves. Both
audits are released with the code, and both exit with a non-zero status when
they detect the condition.
